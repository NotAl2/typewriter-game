# 1. High Concept

A narrative typing game set in the late 1870s that begins as a cozy, atmospheric experience and gradually descends into mystery, psychological horror, and eventually gruesome analog horror.

The player takes the role of a professional **typewriter** whose job is to write letters and documents for people in the local community.

The gameplay initially revolves around:

- Typing letters
- Meeting customers
- Reading newspapers
- Completing timed typing jobs
- Managing a small workspace
- Learning about the people and town around them

Everything initially feels ordinary and peaceful.

Until the player makes a mistake.

An address is typed incorrectly.

The letter is sent anyway.

Days later, another letter arrives.

It comes from the same address.

**An address that does not exist.**

From this point onward, the player's ordinary routine slowly becomes a mystery that eventually turns into something much darker.

---

# 2. Core Gameplay Concept

The game is fundamentally a **typing game**, with the typing mechanic directly integrated into the narrative.

The player receives documents and must type them using their typewriter before the timer expires.

## Basic Gameplay Loop

```text
Receive Document
       ↓
Read Document
       ↓
Type Document
       ↓
Review / Complete
       ↓
Send Letter
       ↓
Receive Payment / Progress
       ↓
Read Newspapers & Other Media
       ↓
Continue Daily Routine
```

As the game progresses, this loop remains familiar while the context surrounding it becomes increasingly disturbing.

The player continues doing the same mundane job while increasingly abnormal events occur around them.

---

# 3. The Cozy Beginning

The game should initially feel like a **cozy historical simulation**.

The player should become comfortable with the world before the horror begins.

## Early Atmosphere

- Warm lighting
- Quiet evenings
- Rain outside
- Wooden furniture
- Typewriter sounds
- Paper and ink
- Newspapers
- Letters from ordinary people
- Friendly customers
- Calm background music
- Small daily routines

The player should feel like they have found a quiet job in a small 1870s community.

The game should encourage the player to settle into a routine.

### Important Design Principle

> **The player should become comfortable before the game takes that comfort away.**

The cozy beginning is not filler.

It establishes the emotional contrast that makes the later horror more effective.

---

# 4. The Initial Incident

The main mystery begins with something incredibly mundane.

The player makes a typing mistake.

An address is entered incorrectly.

The letter is sent.

Nothing immediately happens.

The player continues receiving and typing normal correspondence.

After several ordinary letters, something changes.

A letter arrives from the same incorrect address.

The player recognizes the address.

But there is a problem.

## The Address Does Not Exist.

This becomes the first major mystery of the game.

At first, the player may assume:

- The address was entered incorrectly again.
- Someone is playing a prank.
- The postal service made a mistake.
- The letter was delivered incorrectly.

But more correspondence begins to appear.

The impossible address keeps returning.

---

# 5. Narrative Structure

The story should primarily be told through **written media**.

Rather than relying heavily on exposition, cutscenes, or characters directly explaining the mystery, the player discovers the story by examining documents.

## Possible Narrative Sources

- Letters
- Newspapers
- Telegrams
- Personal notes
- Diaries
- Official reports
- Police documents
- Advertisements
- Notices
- Newspaper obituaries
- Receipts
- Records
- Other period-appropriate documents

Individual pieces of information may initially seem insignificant.

Later documents can change their meaning.

A name mentioned casually in an early letter may become important much later.

A newspaper article may explain something the player witnessed days earlier.

A seemingly meaningless address may eventually become the central location of the entire mystery.

---

# 6. Mystery Progression

The mystery should gradually transition through several stages.

## Stage 1 — Normal

The player is simply doing their job.

Nothing appears supernatural.

## Stage 2 — Strange

Small inconsistencies begin appearing.

- Strange letters
- Repeated names
- Unusual requests
- Contradictory information
- Strange newspaper stories
- Familiar details appearing in unrelated documents

## Stage 3 — Impossible

The player begins encountering things that cannot be explained normally.

- Letters from nonexistent addresses
- Information that should be impossible to know
- Documents referring to events that haven't happened
- Strange recurring symbols
- Disturbing dreams
- Unexplained visions

## Stage 4 — Investigation

The player begins actively investigating the mystery.

The typing job is no longer simply a job.

The documents are clues.

The letters are evidence.

The newspapers contain information.

The player's own experiences may contain clues as well.

## Stage 5 — Horror

The mystery becomes increasingly personal.

The player discovers connections between the impossible correspondence and their own past.

The previously cozy environment becomes increasingly distorted.

---

# 7. Analog Horror

The game should eventually transition into a form of **analog horror**.

The horror should not rely exclusively on traditional jumpscares.

Instead, it should use:

- Distorted documents
- Strange photographs
- Old newspaper clippings
- Missing information
- Contradictory records
- Unexplained audio
- Visual glitches
- Repeated imagery
- Unnatural messages
- Disturbing drawings
- Unreliable memories
- Environmental changes
- Subtle changes to previously familiar locations

The player should frequently question whether something supernatural is actually happening or whether they are witnessing a distorted version of reality.

---

# 8. Black & White Visions

During the story, letters can trigger **black-and-white sketch-style visions**.

These visions are presented differently from the main game.

They could resemble:

- Pencil drawings
- Charcoal sketches
- Rough illustrations
- Old newspaper drawings
- Animated storyboards
- Hand-drawn memories

## Interactive Visions

These sequences should not necessarily be simple cutscenes.

The player can potentially:

- Walk through the environment
- Interact with objects
- Examine clues
- Follow characters
- Perform small actions
- Discover hidden details

The visual style can make these scenes feel like fragments of memories rather than normal gameplay.

---

# 9. Dreams

Dream sequences can exist alongside the visions.

Unlike the visions, dreams can be more abstract and surreal.

They may contain:

- Locations the player hasn't discovered yet
- Familiar people behaving strangely
- Distorted versions of the protagonist's home
- Repeating rooms
- Impossible architecture
- Objects appearing in incorrect places
- Fragmented memories
- Symbols connected to the letters

Dreams should not always be immediately understandable.

Some may only make sense after the player discovers information later in the game.

---

# 10. The Rose Motif

A recurring **rose motif** connects the letters, the mystery, and potentially the protagonist's past.

The rose should gradually become one of the game's most recognizable symbols.

## Letters

Each mysterious letter arrives with:

**One rose petal.**

At first, this may appear insignificant.

The player may simply assume that the petal was accidentally included.

But every subsequent mysterious letter contains another petal.

---

## Progression

```text
Letter 1
   ↓
Rose Petal

Letter 2
   ↓
Rose Petal

Letter 3
   ↓
Rose Petal

...

Final Letter
   ↓
Rose Stem
   ↓
Thorns
```

The final letter does not contain another petal.

It contains the **stem of the rose, complete with thorns**.

This can serve as a major symbolic transition toward the final act of the story.

# 11. Opening Cutscene

## Concept

The game begins with the protagonist sitting quietly at their desk.

A rose is in their hand.

Their wife's final letter is lying open on the desk.

The player begins reading the letter.

As the letter progresses, the protagonist slowly removes petals from the rose.

### Petal Sequence

One petal.

Then another.

Then another.

The room remains calm.

The atmosphere is warm.

The typewriter sits nearby.

The player hears:

- Paper moving
- Quiet breathing
- The rose being handled
- Subtle room ambience

Eventually, only one petal remains.

The protagonist pulls it.

The thorn catches their finger.

A small drop of blood forms.

The blood falls onto the rose.

## Cut to Black.

### Title

**[GAME TITLE]**

### Time Jump

**10 Years Later**

The actual game begins.

# 13. Optional Wife Storyline

In this version of the story:

The protagonist's wife has been dead for ten years.

The circumstances surrounding her death were never properly understood.

Her death was officially considered a suicide.

However, the mysterious letters begin revealing information that suggests something else happened.

The wife may be communicating with the protagonist through the letters.

Rather than simply haunting him, she may be attempting to **guide him toward the truth**.

---

# 14. Optional Hidden House

The impossible address eventually leads the protagonist toward a location connected to his wife's death.

The address does not appear to exist through normal records.

However, following the clues eventually reveals a **hidden house**.

The house may contain:

- Evidence
- Personal belongings
- Documents
- Drawings
- Memories
- Information about the wife's death
- Clues about the mysterious correspondence
- Evidence pointing toward a killer

The house becomes one of the major locations in the final portion of the game.

---

# 15. Optional Killer Mystery

One possible central mystery is:

> **Who killed the protagonist's wife?**

The protagonist believes he is investigating the murder of his wife.

The letters guide him toward evidence.

Eventually, he may discover the identity of the killer.

However, an additional twist is being considered.

## Possible Twist

**The protagonist himself was the killer.**

He does not remember committing the crime.

Possible explanations could include:

- Lost memories
- Repressed memories
- Psychological trauma
- Manipulation
- An unexplained supernatural event
- An unreliable perception of his own past

This twist is **not currently confirmed**.

If used, the entire story would need to be structured around carefully planted clues that can be interpreted differently before and after the reveal.

---

# 16. Possible Endings

## Ending A — The Truth

The protagonist successfully uncovers what happened to his wife.

Inside the hidden house, he encounters a depiction or apparition of her.

He approaches her.

They embrace.

She gradually begins to disappear.

Her body transforms into **rose petals**.

The petals fall around him.

The scene ends quietly.

---

## Ending B — The Killer

If the protagonist is revealed to have killed his wife, the ending would be substantially different.

The exact ending is currently undecided.

Possible directions include:

- Confrontation with his own memories
- The wife revealing the truth
- The protagonist realizing what he has done
- A supernatural punishment
- The protagonist becoming trapped in the house
- An ambiguous ending

**This ending is currently an open design question.**

---

# 17. Memory & Reality

An important question for the game is whether the protagonist can trust his own memories.

The visions, dreams, letters, and documents can intentionally create uncertainty.

The player may encounter multiple versions of the same event.

Some information may contradict what they previously believed.

This allows the game to maintain ambiguity around:

- What actually happened
- What the protagonist remembers
- What the wife is trying to communicate
- Whether the supernatural events are real
- Whether the protagonist is being manipulated
- Whether the protagonist is responsible for the events

---

# 18. Horror Progression

The game should follow a gradual progression rather than immediately becoming horrifying.

### Phase 1 — Cozy

**Feeling:**

> Warm, nostalgic, peaceful.

Gameplay focuses on ordinary typing work and daily life.

---

### Phase 2 — Uneasy

**Feeling:**

> Something feels slightly wrong.

The player begins encountering strange documents and inconsistencies.

---

### Phase 3 — Mysterious

**Feeling:**

> Someone is trying to tell you something.

The impossible correspondence begins.

The player starts investigating.

---

### Phase 4 — Disturbing

**Feeling:**

> You shouldn't be reading this.

The player discovers increasingly disturbing information.

Visions and dreams become more frequent.

---

### Phase 5 — Gruesome

**Feeling:**

> You were never supposed to find this.

The mystery becomes deeply personal.

The hidden house and the truth behind the letters are revealed.

---

### Phase 6 — Resolution

**Feeling:**

> You finally understand.

The player confronts the truth and reaches one of the possible endings.

---

# 19. Gameplay & Story Integration

The typing mechanic should remain relevant throughout the story.

The player should not feel like they are playing a typing minigame between story scenes.

Instead:

> **The typing is how the player experiences the story.**

The documents themselves should contain:

- Clues
- Character development
- Worldbuilding
- Red herrings
- Foreshadowing
- Mysteries
- Contradictions

Typing a letter should feel like an actual part of the protagonist's life.

- Paper
- Rain
- Fireplace
- Clock ticking
- Quiet room ambience
- Soft music
- Street sounds

## Later Game

- Typewriter keys continuing when nobody is typing
- A carriage bell at the wrong time
- Clock sounds becoming distorted
- Distant footsteps
- Unexplained knocking
- Voices hidden within environmental sounds

The goal is to make familiar sounds become frightening.

---

# 25. Design Philosophy

### Cozy First

Give the player time to love the world.

### Horror Later

Slowly take that comfort away.

### Let the Player Discover

Avoid explaining everything directly.

### Every Document Can Matter

Even mundane writing can contain clues.

### Small Details Should Reward Attention

Players who carefully read and remember things should discover more.

### Make the Familiar Unfamiliar

Previously harmless objects and locations should become unsettling through context.

### Don't Overuse Jumpscares

The primary source of horror should be **anticipation, mystery, realization, and disturbing discovery**.

# 30. Final Vision

The goal is to create a game where the player starts by enjoying the simple pleasure of:

**sitting at a desk, listening to rain, and typing letters.**

By the end, that same desk, that same typewriter, and those same letters should feel completely different.

The player should look back at the beginning of the game and realize that the clues were there all along.

> **The game begins with a letter.**
>
> **It ends with the truth.**
